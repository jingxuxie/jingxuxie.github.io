How simulations help
a real robot learn?

We test whether simulated demonstrations help an arm place a box at targets it has rarely seen in real dataset.

Jingxu Xie • ACT & π0.5

THE QUESTION

Can the arm succeed
an unseen target position?

The task is simple: pick up the blue box and place it on the white paper. We move the paper between left and right to test whether the arm follows the target or repeats its training behavior.

Main results
  • Success in simulation does not guarantee success on the real robot, even when the scenes look similar.
  • Combining simulated and real demonstrations improves performance when real data is limited.
  • Broader training coverage helps the robot succeed across different target positions.

02 / TEST ON THE REAL ARM

The target moves left.
The arm still goes right.

Real-right and real-left are real demonstrations with the paper on the right or left. Sim-right and sim-left are simulated demonstrations of those tasks. Left and right refer to the paper's position in the front-camera view.

First, we train on real-right + sim-right + sim-left, with no real-left data. The hope is to transfer the left-target behavior learned in simulation to the real arm. Yet in the real test below, the arm moves right even when the paper is on the left, and the arm moves correctly in simulation, suggesting that the policy still responds differently to real and simulated images, despite their visual similarity.

A POSSIBLE EXPLANATION · STABLE HOVERING WITH NOISE

The model may aim for the wrong position.

Why might adding a few left demonstrations hurt right-side performance? This toy model offers one possible explanation: the policy learns to aim between the targets. Position feedback then keeps it near that middle point, even with noise. This is a hypothesis, not a diagnosis of ACT.

1. The model may learn the scene instead of the target.

The model has two pieces of information: where the paper is, and what the surrounding scene looks like. If left targets always appear in one type of scene and right targets in another, either can explain the demonstrations. The model may use both to choose a destination instead of relying only on the paper's position.

The toy represents these as two numbers: G is the paper's side, and Z is the side usually associated with that scene. Both are −1 for left or +1 for right. These are simplified inputs, not actual camera images.

With only right examples, the model can always choose right. A few left examples rule out that answer, but may not reveal which information to trust. In this toy, the fit gives both equal weight and predicts:

m ≈ ½G + ½Z

Here m is the chosen destination. If the paper is on the right (G = +1), but the scene looks like the left examples (Z = −1), the result is zero: the middle. The same problem can happen on either side.

2. The controller keeps returning to the middle.

The controller moves toward its chosen destination and corrects small movements away from it. In the simplified case without noise or speed limiting:

next error = 0.8 × current error

“Error” means the distance from the chosen destination, not from the paper. Each step reduces it by 20%. If the model chose the middle, the controller keeps returning there. The tests below add noise and small offsets to check whether this hovering persists.

How to read the figure

The paper and scene suggest opposite directions. A and C put the paper on the left in a scene associated with right-side examples. B and D reverse this. Positions and noise levels are toy numbers, not centimeters or camera pixels.

The top row compares three ways to use noisy inputs: keep their values, reduce them to left/right choices, or use those choices after dropping uncertain training examples. Dropping examples is a training step, not a rule that tells the controller to stop.

More diverse examples can remove the hover.

Examples with the same background but different paper positions help the model learn where to place the box: on the paper, regardless of the background. In the main test, the small left subset has no such examples; the full left set adds four. Varied simulation also adds them, allowing the model to learn m ≈ G. Simulation that repeats the old combinations does not resolve the problem.

When the paper and scene suggest opposite directions, the small-data model hovers in 97.3% of left trials and 97.8% of right trials at noise level 0.4. With varied simulation, it reaches the target in 99.2% and 99.5% of those trials. Success also remains near 99% when the paper and scene suggest the same side.

A “left-like scene” resembles the scenes associated with left targets in training; a “right-like scene” means the reverse. Either side can fail.

05 / TAKEAWAYS

Simulation helps.
Real-world tests still matter.

Match more than appearance

Similar camera views can still lead to different behavior. Visual matching alone does not close the sim-to-real gap.

Combine real and simulated data

Simulation improves real-arm success when paired with a small set of real demonstrations.

Test beyond this setup

Next, test new object positions, lighting, and camera views to see how well the gains transfer.
